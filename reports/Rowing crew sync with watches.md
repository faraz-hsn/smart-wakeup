# Turn the crew's own watches into a sync meter

**Bottom line: this is a good hackathon idea and a credible second choice, but it is not a clear winner over Stair Snack HRR.** It scores **29/40** on the same scorecard used for the earlier ideas, which puts it second behind Stair Snack HRR (33) and just ahead of Orthostatic Morning Check (28). Nothing on the market does exactly this. Crews that want per-rower timing today pay for instrumented oarlocks (NK EmPower costs **$499 per seat** plus a $469 head unit). The cheap tools (phone apps, GPS boxes, Garmin's own rowing mode) only see the boat as a whole, and no Connect IQ or Apple Watch app turns the crew's own watches into a sync system. The idea is not new in principle, though. A **2024 ACM paper used smartwatch accelerometers to show timing differences between rowing teammates**, and several **2025 US patents claim per-rower stroke-sync systems**, so the pitch must acknowledge both. The science supports crew timing as something real and trainable. Elite crews catch within about **11–20 ms** of each other. The evidence that tighter sync makes a boat *faster* is mixed, and there is **no evidence that sync prevents injury**. The hard technical truth is that a wrist sensor times strokes with roughly **±30 ms per-stroke error**. That is the same size as the differences elite crews care about, so the honest product is "rhythm and gross-desync feedback for club crews", averaged over many strokes, not elite biomechanics. On a Fenix 7 Pro, everything for a **"record on each watch, align on a laptop" MVP** is documented and available: 100 Hz accelerometer and gyroscope, millisecond timestamps, raw sensor logging into FIT files. A **live watch-to-watch link** over ANT exists in the API, but nobody has publicly shown it working between two watches, so it is a stretch goal only. The biggest risk is not technical but thematic: judges asked for something that improves *your own* health and wellness, and a crew-sync tool reads as sports performance unless the pitch leads with the "rowers' high", group-exercise stress and adherence evidence.

## Rowing words you need before reading on

A rowing **stroke** has four parts. The **catch** is the moment the blade enters the water at the start of the stroke, with the rower compressed forward on the sliding seat. The **drive** is the push with legs, back and arms that moves the boat. The **finish** (also called the release) is when the blade comes out of the water with the handle near the chest. The **recovery** is the slide forward to the next catch. **Stroke rate** is strokes per minute (spm); training is usually 18–24 spm and racing 32–40+. In a boat, seats are numbered from the bow. The **stroke seat** is the rower nearest the stern, who sets the rhythm everyone else follows. A **cox** steers and calls the rhythm in bigger boats ("8+" means eight rowers plus cox). An **erg** is a rowing machine, such as a Concept2. **Sweep** rowers hold one oar each, alternating port and starboard sides. A **sync offset** is how many milliseconds a given rower's catch (or finish) comes before or after the stroke seat's. That number is what this app would measure: one offset per rower, per stroke.

The team's idea differs on three points, and all three belong in the pitch. First, it **uses watches people already own**: an EmPower eight costs roughly $4,000 in oarlocks before the head unit, while a crew of Garmin owners pays nothing extra. Second, it is **per-rower**, which the cheap tier is not. A Concept2 forum rower describes the real-world gap: in a quad usually only stroke seat has a sensor, so they strap their own device to a rigger and review afterwards ([C2 forum](https://www.c2forum.com/viewtopic.php?p=540661)). Third, it is **low cost and works indoors and out**, including on ergs, where "rowing in time" today is judged only by eye. One naming collision to know about: an iOS app called "CrewSync – Row Smarter" exists, but it does seat racing and PM5 data, not stroke synchronization ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id6763919895)). Pick a different name.

### Prior art the team must acknowledge

Two things make the idea *not new in principle*, and judges with a research background may know them. The closest precedent is an academic one. **"A System to Visualize Differences in Paddling Timing between Teammates in Rowing"** (ACM, 2024, UbiComp/ISWC companion, DOI 10.1145/3675094.3678441) proposes exactly the core mechanism: smartwatch accelerometers on teammates, visualized as timing differences ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3675094.3678441)). Only the abstract snippet was available, so its accuracy and hardware are unknown, and no commercial product from it surfaced. The second is a cluster of patents. **US 12,409,377 B1, "System and method for synchronized rowing"** (filed January 2025, issued September 2025) focuses on catch and finish timing ([USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12409377)). **US 12,403,357 B1** claims a central unit, sensors that detect stroke timings, and rower units that deliver sync status to each rower ([USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12403357)), and **US 12,594,482** covers centrally controlled rower synchronization with personalized performance data ([USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12594482)). Older patents cover the same ground: **US 11,298,590** describes timing differences against a reference rower with visual, audio or tactile cues ([Justia](https://patents.justia.com/patent/11298590)), and **US 10,252,134** ("Paddle link") tells a paddler who is off-timing ([USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10252134)). The full patent claims were not read. Patents do not matter for a hackathon, but anyone commercializing this later needs a proper freedom-to-operate check. The honest framing is: "Researchers have shown the concept with smartwatches and patents claim the system; nobody ships it on the watches crews already wear."

## Crew timing is real and trainable, but faster boats are not proven

Research treats crew rowing as an archetype of coordination between people, and the timing numbers are small. In elite crews, sync is tighter at the catch (about **11 ms**, or 3.8% of the stroke's water phase) than at the finish (about **21 ms**). Timing spread grows in big boats (quads and eights) and shrinks at higher stroke rates ([ResearchGate, elite crews force-pattern study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11547038_Dynamics_of_coordination_within_elite_rowing_crews_Evidence_from_force_pattern_analysis); [J Hum Kinet kayak video](https://johk.pl/?p=4841)). Sync can be trained: a coxless pair rowed with tighter catch timing and closer oar angles after six weeks of crew training ([Feigean et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01139/pdf)), and the NK Drexel case study shows an eight cutting its catch-angle spread from about 4° to 2° with per-seat feedback ([Junior Rowing News](https://juniorrowingnews.com/tracking-improvements-the-empower-oarlock)). Pulling these together, a **10–50 ms offset band** is the zone that matters for good crews. This is a synthesis, not a published standard. A student oar-sensor project used a ±50 ms window around the lead rower's catch as "on time" ([Notre Dame report](https://seniordesign.ee.nd.edu/2019/Design%20Teams/row/documents/FinalReport.pdf)).

**The evidence that tighter sync makes the boat faster is mixed**, and the team should not claim it. In Baudouin & Hawkins' instrumented pairs, timing differences contributed at most about 6.7% of the synchrony measure; differences in force *magnitude* dominated, and performance could not be predicted from power plus synchrony ([Baudouin & Hawkins 2004](https://smithengineering.queensu.ca/mme/faculty/deluzio/jam/files/Baudouin.pdf)). Fifteen double sculls with a hull IMU showed that better sync gave **less roll but more surge, heave and pitch** fluctuation ([Cuijpers et al. 2017](https://read.qxmd.com/read/27882632/rocking-the-boat-does-perfect-rowing-crew-synchronization-reduce-detrimental-boat-movements)). Pairs rowing in *antiphase* (alternating) had smaller speed fluctuations, worth roughly 5–6% of power ([Cuijpers et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4505883)). Kleshnev's case study of two lightweight doubles found the more efficient crew had *worse* handle timing (20 ms vs 18 ms) but nearly identical seat timing (under 2 ms vs 35 ms), which suggests body-mass timing matters more than hand timing ([Kleshnev case study](https://rowperfect.co.uk/rowing-efficiency-case-study)). Expert rowers also say they mostly coordinate with the boat, not by watching each other ([Millar thesis](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/7560)). The defensible target is therefore "**consistent, matched rhythm**", not "zero milliseconds".

**There is no evidence that crew sync prevents injury.** Rib stress fractures are the signature rowing injury (8–16% of elite rowers). Technique is one proposed risk factor among many, prevention strategies are unproven, and the relevant technique variables (such as seat-versus-handle velocity) are not measurable from a wrist ([McDonnell et al. 2011](https://www.pubmed.gov/21985212/)). No study linking desynchronization to injury was found. Leave injury claims out entirely, or limit them to training-load monitoring (stroke counts and volume).

The strongest evidence for real-time sync *feedback* comes from sound, not vibration. Live sonification of boat acceleration made junior crews row significantly faster and narrowed the spread of timing parameters between rowers, without changing power, lactate or HR ([Schaffert & Mattes 2016](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4041710?lng=en)), though most of this work comes from one group. Haptic feedback for rowing timing has weak or null results ([BIO Web of Conferences 2011](https://www.bio-conferences.org/10.1051/bioconf/20110100025); [RERO](https://doc.rero.ch/record/333015?ln=en)), and no study tests watch vibration for crew sync. A vibration cue is a fine demo, but present it as untested.

### A wrist times strokes to about ±30 ms, the same size as the signal

Garmin's wrist stroke *rate* is reliable. A Forerunner 735XT agreed with a Catapult unit within about **1 spm** over 118 on-water sessions ([Watts et al. 2024](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4089934)), although forum users report missed strokes at low rates and spikes to 150 spm at lap changes ([Erg IQ forum](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/showcase/4281/erg-iq/50322); [FR955 forum](https://forums.garmin.com/sports-fitness/running-multisport/f/forerunner-955-series/335134/spikes-in-stroke-rate-of-rowing-activities-after-15-19)). Per-stroke *timing* is much harder. The only validation found used one athlete on an erg: arm-worn IMUs had an average event error of about **29 ms**, with the catch detected **46–60 ms early** ([ISBS 2021](https://commons.nmu.edu/isbs/vol39/iss1/23)). A kayak stroke-onset detector managed 24.6 ± 51.6 ms ([SciTePress 2025](https://www.scitepress.org/publishedPapers/2025/137812/pdf/index.html)). An open-source hobby project trying crew sync with phone IMUs is still at **77–94 ms** error against a 30 ms target ([wrc-coach PR #8](https://github.com/thiesmoeller/wrc-coach/pull/8)). No study validates on-water smartwatch catch timing against oar sensors.

Two facts make this workable rather than fatal. A *systematic* bias (catch read 50 ms early) mostly cancels when every watch runs the same algorithm, because the app compares rowers to each other. The *random* jitter does not cancel, but it shrinks when you average: report a **rolling offset over 10–20 strokes**, not single strokes. With that, the app can reliably spot a rower who is consistently 60–100+ ms late or early, which is common in novice and club crews, and is honestly blind to the 10–20 ms refinements elite crews chase. The wrist is also not the blade: it tracks hand movement, which is a proxy for blade timing.

## Fenix 7 Pro has every MVP building block; the live link is the gamble

Every part of a record-then-align system is documented in the local SDK 9.1.0 docs and supported on the Fenix 7 Pro (device group API 5.2). The constraints below shape the design.

**Sensors and sampling.** The Fenix 7 Pro supports **100 Hz accelerometer and 100 Hz gyroscope** (50 Hz magnetometer) through `Sensor.registerSensorDataListener`, delivered in batches of up to 4 s with **millisecond timestamps** (`:includeTimestamps`). Only one listener registration is allowed at a time, and calling it from a data field crashes the app, so this must be a **device (watch) app** (`SDK/doc/Toybox/Sensor.html`; `DEV/simulator.json`). At 100 Hz the sample interval is 10 ms, so sampling is not the bottleneck; biomechanics is. `Math.FirFilter`/`IirFilter` exist for cheap filtering. Garmin's native rowing stroke rate is **not exposed** to Connect IQ (`Activity.Info` has no rowing field), so the team writes its own stroke detector (`SDK/doc/Toybox/Activity/Info.html`).

**Recording.** A watch app can create a FIT session with `sport = ROWING`, attach a `SensorLogging.SensorLogger` that writes raw accelerometer and gyroscope samples into the same FIT file, and add custom fields with `FitContributor` (`SDK/doc/Toybox/ActivityRecording.html`; `SDK/doc/Toybox/SensorLogging/SensorLogger.html`). FIT record fields update about once per second, so per-stroke timing lives in the raw logged samples, processed offline. The exact FIT message layout of SensorLogger output was not confirmed; parse a test file early.

**Clocks.** This is the hardest problem. `System.getTimer()` is a millisecond counter that starts at boot, so it differs on every watch. Wall-clock time (`ClockTime`) and GPS time (`Position.Info.when`) resolve only to **whole seconds** (`SDK/doc/Toybox/System.html`; `SDK/doc/Toybox/System/ClockTime.html`). So the watches cannot be aligned to tens of milliseconds from their clocks. Alignment must come from a shared physical event. The recommended trick is a **"watch bump"**: rowers knock their watches together (or all slap the same gunwale or erg rail) at the start and end of a piece. That creates a sharp spike in every accelerometer log. Aligning those spikes gives each watch's offset, and the end bump corrects for clock drift by linear interpolation. Drift rate on the Fenix is undocumented, so the end bump is essential. On water, a second refinement is possible: everyone sits in one rigid hull, so the boat's surge appears in every wrist's signal and can be cross-correlated. That is an unvalidated idea, because arm motion dominates and could bias the lag by exactly the sync error being measured. It also does not work on separate ergs. Use the bump as the primary method.

**Watch-to-watch radio.** BLE in Connect IQ is **central-only** (no advertising or peripheral role), so two watches cannot talk over BLE (`SDK/doc/Toybox/BluetoothLowEnergy.html`; [forum feature request](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/444504/feature-request-support-ble-gap-advertising-broadcaster-in-connect-iq-for-camera-wake-iot)). The phone path is too slow: round trips of about **1.5–2 s** on a Fenix 7 Pro, and messages are lost above about 1 per second ([Fenix 7 Pro thread](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/447091/supported-low-latency-companion-transport-alongside-garmin-connect-fenix-7-pro-android); [BLE rate thread](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/162925/maximum-amount-of-messages-per-second-over-ble-connection)). The only direct path is the **ANT generic channel**. One watch opens a master channel and broadcasts 8-byte messages at a set rate (`messagePeriod = 32768 / Hz`, so 8192 means 4 Hz). Others open slave channels and receive them (`SDK/doc/Toybox/Ant/GenericChannel.html`; [forum: custom ANT broadcast](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/411246/custom-ant-broadcast-using-genericchannel)). Eight bytes at 4 Hz is plenty for "stroke N caught at t ms" but not for raw IMU streaming. The catches: **no one has publicly shown two Garmin watches talking over CIQ ANT on real hardware**. Developers report that generic channels connect slowly and drop on Fenix 6 Pro and Edge 830 while working perfectly in the simulator, and that only one app per watch can use a generic channel ([bug report](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/i/bug-reports/connect-iq-ant-apps-are-very-weak-not-usable-compared-to-garmin-ant-native-apps); [interval thread](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/323376/about-the-interval-between-ant-transmissions)). An old thread puts the ceiling at about **8 generic channels**, minus those the watch itself uses ([forum](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/discussion/56/how-many-generic-ant-channels-are-allowed)). A live eight-rower hub is at or past that limit. A two-watch live link is the realistic stretch.

**Battery and water.** The Fenix 7 Pro is 10 ATM water-rated, uses buttons (fine with wet hands), and lasts up to 57 h in GPS-only mode, so a 1–2 h session is no problem ([the5krunner specs](https://the5krunner.com/specs/garmin/fenix-7-pro/)). Only one watch needs GPS.

### Each sensor gets a real job

The bonus rewards sensors put to real use. Four earn their place. The compass does not, for now.

## Build "record on each watch, align on a laptop", then try a two-watch live link

The recommended architecture keeps every risky part off the critical path. Each watch runs the same Connect IQ device app. The app has one screen with a start/stop button, a seat-number setting, a live stroke counter and stroke rate (from its own detector, which proves detection works), and HR. On start it creates a ROWING FIT session with a SensorLogger capturing 100 Hz accelerometer and gyroscope, enables GPS on the stroke seat's watch only, and prompts "bump watches". A FitContributor field stores the stroke count and the last catch time each second. After the piece, everyone bumps again and stops. FIT files come off the watches over USB, which avoids the phone entirely. A Python script on the laptop parses each file, finds the two bump spikes and maps every watch onto the stroke seat's timeline, detects catches and finishes per rower, and computes each rower's offset per stroke. It then reports a rolling 10-stroke mean and spread, the percentage of strokes within ±50 ms, and a crew sync score. The output is a per-stroke timeline (one row per rower, dots at each catch), an offset chart per seat, and HR and boat speed underneath. Bands of **green under 50 ms, amber 50–100 ms and red over 100 ms** fit the wrist's real accuracy.

| Time | MVP work (record then align) |
|---|---|
| 0–2 h | Start from SDK samples `RecordSample` and `PitchCounter`. Build the watch app: start/stop, ROWING session with SensorLogger, 100 Hz accel+gyro listener, simple on-watch stroke counter, FitContributor field |
| 2–4 h | Record test pieces on 2–3 watches (erg or miming), with bumps and a slow-motion phone video as ground truth. Pull FIT files over USB |
| 4–7 h | Python: parse, align on bumps, detect catches, compute offsets and sync score, plot. Tune the detector against the video |
| 7–9 h | Stretch: live ANT link between two watches. Polish the demo and slides in parallel |

**Stretch goal: live link between two watches with a vibration cue.** The stroke seat's watch opens an ANT master channel and broadcasts at about 4 Hz. Each message carries a counter, the master's `getTimer()` value and the time of its last catch. The follower's watch opens a slave channel and records its own `getTimer()` on each receipt. It estimates the clock offset as the minimum gap over many beacons (the minimum filters out delivery delays), converts the leader's catch times into its own time, and compares them with its own catches. If the rolling offset passes a threshold (say 80 ms late), it vibrates. That is a stroke-to-stroke correction, not a within-stroke one. Copy the channel setup from the SDK's `GenericChannelBurst` sample (master TX-only, slave RX-only, public network, a non-default frequency). Timebox it. If two real watches do not exchange a packet within an hour, drop it and present it as "next step". Scaling live to eight rowers is out of scope for one day.

### The demo should show a deliberate desync, then a fix

The strongest stage demo uses **two or three teammates wearing Fenix watches**, on ergs if the venue has them, or simply miming the rowing motion seated side by side. Any repeated synchronized movement works too, such as arm swings to a beat. Play a metronome at about 24 spm. In round one, one teammate deliberately rows a fraction late. In round two, everyone follows the stroke person closely. Bump, stop, plug in, run the script. The audience sees the timeline with the late rower's dots trailing and their seat in red, then round two turning green, alongside each person's HR. If the live ANT stretch works, add a 30-second clip of the follower's watch buzzing when they drift. Show a pre-recorded on-water or erg session as a backup in case anything fails live. Close with a slide showing the same pipeline for dragon boat or a group fitness class to show the wider reach.

### Test these on real watches first

The simulator hides exactly the failure modes that matter here (ANT drops, timing jitter), so the first two hours should go to hardware checks, in this order.

| # | Test on real Fenix 7 Pro hardware | Pass criterion | If it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 Hz accel+gyro listener and SensorLogger running together for 10 minutes | No crash, no watchdog error, samples in the FIT file | Use SensorLogger only and detect strokes offline |
| 2 | Parse a SensorLogger FIT file in Python | Raw accel/gyro samples with usable timestamps come out | Store a compact catch list in app storage instead |
| 3 | Bump alignment repeatability: bump twice, 5 minutes apart, on two watches | Both bumps give the same offset within ~10–20 ms after drift correction | Use more bumps or a shared tap on one surface |
| 4 | Catch detection vs ground truth: 20 strokes filmed with phone slow-motion (240 fps, ~4 ms per frame) | Consistent bias, per-stroke scatter around 30 ms or better | Switch to gyro-based direction reversal, or report rate and rhythm only |
| 5 | ANT master/slave between two Fenix 7 Pros (timeboxed 1 hour) | Packets arrive steadily at 4 Hz over 5+ metres for 5 minutes | Drop the live stretch and present it as a future step |
| 6 | HR during a recorded session with the sensor listener active | 1 Hz HR present throughout | Leave HR out of live display; read it from the FIT afterwards |
| 7 | Vibration while the sensor listener runs | Vibration does not corrupt stroke detection (forum users saw spikes from lap vibration) | Mask detection for 200 ms after a cue |

The vibration test matters because a Venu 2 user traced stroke-rate spikes to the lap vibration itself ([Garmin forum](https://forums.garmin.com/sports-fitness/healthandwellness/f/venu-2-plus-964331130/367998/venu-2-plus-stroke-rate-spikes-when-rowing-on-water)).

## Pitch it as the "rowers' high" you can see, not as a racing tool

The theme is "your *own* health and wellness", and a crew-sync tool reads at first as sports performance. That is the main judging risk, rated **moderate**. The fix is to lead with personal wellbeing and present sync as the mechanism. The evidence below is real but mostly small, so each claim should be stated at its true strength.

| Wellness claim | Key evidence | Evidence quality | How to say it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rowing in sync feels better ("rowers' high") | 12 Oxford rowers: synchronized group erg sessions raised pain threshold (an endorphin proxy) more than rowing alone at equal power ([Cohen et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2817271)). Larger effect with teammates than strangers ([JEMS](https://scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/323)). 264 students: synchrony and exertion each raised pain threshold and bonding ([Tarr et al. 2015](https://www.anthro.ox.ac.uk/node/865641)) | Low–moderate: small samples, indirect proxy | "Research links moving in sync to an endorphin response" |
| Synchrony builds social connection | Meta-analysis of 42 studies (N = 4,327): medium effect on prosocial behaviour, smaller on bonding and mood ([Mogan et al. 2017](https://www.pubmed.gov/28105388/)). But effects shrink or vanish with blinded experimenters and may reflect expectancy ([PMC9987344](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9987344/)) | Contested | "Associated with", never "proven" |
| Group exercise lowers stress more than solo | 69 medical students: group exercisers cut perceived stress by 26%; solo exercisers did not ([Yorks et al. 2017](https://thedo.osteopathic.org/2017/10/study-finds-group-exercise-reduces-stress-solo-workouts/)) | Low: not randomized, author tied to Les Mills | Use as supporting, not headline |
| A shared score keeps people showing up | Group programmes with team-building beat standard classes and home exercise ([Burke et al. 2006](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/2920673/BurkeNtoumanisGroupIndividualApproach.pdf)); team-building is linked to cohesion and adherence ([Bruner & Spink 2010](https://g4yd.nipissingu.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/72/2014/03/Bruner-Spink.2010.GD_1.pdf)) | Moderate, mixed | "Team feedback is a known adherence lever" |
| Health communities that paddle in sync | Breast-cancer-survivor dragon boat: roughly 345–400 teams in about 40 countries ([Wikipedia](https://wikiwand.com/en/articles/Breast_cancer_survivors'_dragon_boating); [BCP Team USA](https://sites.google.com/view/bcpteamusa/our-story)). Lymphoedema fell from 21% to 4% in a German cohort of 28 paddlers ([PMC10110661](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10110661/)) | Moderate for dragon boating itself; **no evidence that sync feedback adds to it** | "Where we go next", not a claim about the app |
| Prevents injury | None found | None | Do not claim |

British Rowing already uses the rowers'-high research to argue for training with clubmates rather than alone ([British Rowing](https://www.britishrowing.org/2020/03/training-is-it-better-to-exercise-alone-or-online-with-your-clubmates/)), which makes it a credible hook. Note what is missing: no study shows that *feedback on* sync improves wellbeing. The literature covers synchrony itself.

A pitch that holds up: "Training with your crew feels better than training alone, and research backs that up: moving in sync is linked to an endorphin response and lower stress. But club crews get no feedback on whether they are really moving together, because sensors cost about $500 a seat. We use the watches already on our wrists. Each of us sees our own timing offset, our effort and HR next to the crew's, and a sync score we improve together, so we get more of the 'rowers' high' and keep showing up." Then make the "own" angle concrete: every rower gets a personal view (their offset, consistency, HR versus the crew), not just the coach. Generalize at the end with dragon boat survivor crews, group fitness classes and running groups, and keep the demo on rowing motion. Two framing traps to avoid: do not lead with "faster boats" (mixed evidence and off-theme), and do not claim elite precision (the wrist cannot deliver it). Existing demand is real but small. A Garmin forum request for an app that connects team members and compares their training metrics went unanswered for years, with a later reply that an elite team "would love" it ([Garmin forum](https://forums.garmin.com/developer/connect-iq/f/app-ideas/214635/row-team-training-metrics/1030332)). The market is niche (British Rowing has roughly 32,000 members), so the case for scale rests on the platform reaching dragon boat and group fitness ([British Rowing Annual Review](https://britishrowing.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/British-Rowing-2023-24-Annual-Review.pdf)).

## Should we pick this? It scores 29/40, second to Stair Snack

Same 1–5 scale and criteria as the earlier evaluation (5 = best for the team; "gap" 5 = nothing exists).

| Idea | Demand evidence | Gap vs native (F7 Pro) | Gap vs CIQ apps | Feasibility | Sensors (real) | Demo | 1-day build | Judging appeal | **Total /40** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stair Snack + HRR trainer | 2 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 5 | **33** |
| **Crew Sync (record and align MVP)** | 3 | 5 | 5 | 3 | 4 — accel, gyro, GPS, HR | 3 | 3 | 3 | **29** |
| Orthostatic Morning Check | 2 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 4 | **28** |
| Wind-Down Coach (F7-class) | 4 | 4 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 3 | **27** |

The scores rest on these judgments. **Demand gets 3**: there is first-hand evidence (an unanswered Garmin forum request, a rower strapping their own sensor to a rigger, hobbyists building their own) but from a niche population. **Both gap scores get 5**: no Garmin device and no CIQ app does multi-watch sync. **Feasibility gets 3**: the MVP uses documented APIs, but per-stroke wrist timing is unvalidated and the live link is unproven. **Sensors get 4**: four sensors with distinct jobs. **Demo gets 3**: a multi-person "one rower is late" reveal is memorable, but it needs several watches and a laptop pipeline that works on stage. **One-day build gets 3**: it needs a watch app *and* an analysis pipeline. **Judging appeal gets 3**: novel and fun, but the theme fit has to be argued. If judging leans heavily on a short live demo (Demo, Build and Appeal weighted ×1.5), Crew Sync drops to about 33.5 against Stair Snack's 40, and Orthostatic (34) overtakes it. The idea becomes clearly worth picking if the team has two or three Fenix watches available on the day, at least one teammate who rows or paddles (which makes the "our own wellness" claim true rather than borrowed), and access to an erg.

## Final verdict and top reasons

**Verdict: pick it only if the team rows and has 2–3 watches on the day; otherwise Stair Snack HRR remains the safer winner.** As a concept it is strong, original on the Garmin platform, and honest if pitched as rhythm feedback. As a hackathon entry it carries more build and theme risk than the top pick.

1. **It fills a real gap on the platform.** Per-rower timing today costs roughly $500+ per seat, and no Garmin, CIQ or Apple Watch product turns the crew's own watches into a sync system. Credit the 2024 ACM smartwatch paper and the 2025 patents up front.
2. **The MVP is buildable in a day.** It relies only on documented Fenix 7 Pro features: 100 Hz accel/gyro, FIT logging, a "watch bump" for alignment and a laptop script. The ANT live link is the gamble. Timebox it.
3. **The science supports "rhythm", not "speed" or "safety".** Crew timing is real and trainable, but faster-boat evidence is mixed and injury evidence is absent. Wrist error (~30 ms per stroke) means averaging over strokes and presenting green/amber/red bands, not elite millisecond claims.
4. **The sensor story is genuine.** Accelerometer, gyroscope, GPS and HR each do a distinct job. Leave the compass out unless a watch rides on the hull.
5. **Theme fit is the deciding risk.** Lead with the rowers' high, group-exercise stress relief and adherence, flagged as small-study evidence, and give each rower a personal view. If the judges cannot hear "my own wellness" within the first 20 seconds, the idea loses to Stair Snack.
